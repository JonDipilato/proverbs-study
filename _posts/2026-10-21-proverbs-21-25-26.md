There's a trap in this proverb that's easy to miss because it sounds like it's just about being lazy. It isn't only that. It's about what wanting does to a man who never turns the want into work. The desire itself — *taavath* — doesn't go away just because nothing's being done about it. It sits there, running, all day long, exactly like verse 26 says. And a desire that never gets worked for doesn't fade out quietly. It kills him. Not because wanting things is evil, but because a want with no work behind it eats a man alive from the inside while his hands sit still.

And his hands aren't tied. That's the part worth sitting with. *Me'anu* — they refuse. Nobody forced his hands to be idle. He chose it, the same way you choose anything else, except this choice gets dressed up as fatigue or bad luck or "just not feeling it today." It's none of those things. It's a decision, made quietly, over and over, to want instead of work.

Then the proverb sets the righteous man right next to him, same chapter, same breath, doing the opposite with the same body part. The sluggard's hand refuses to labor. The righteous man's hand refuses to *yachsokh* — refuses to hold back. He gives. Not because he's got some surplus the sluggard doesn't. Because he decided his hand would move instead of sit.

That's really the whole choice in front of you today. Not "am I lazy" in some abstract sense — almost nobody thinks of themselves that way. The real question is narrower: is there a desire you've been carrying around all day that you still haven't turned into work? And is there a hand you've been keeping closed that could open today instead?
