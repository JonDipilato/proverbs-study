It would be easy to read this proverb as plain advice — don't hang around jerks — and move on. But look at how precisely it's built. It doesn't say the angry man will yell at you or ruin your day. It says you'll *become* him. That's a different kind of warning, and it's the one most people miss, because nobody thinks they're the kind of person who picks up someone else's temper. You just think you're being loyal, or polite, or that you can handle being around him without it touching you.

The Hebrew won't let you off that easy. The angry man is *ba'al aph* — the owner of his anger, not a victim of a rough moment. And what he owns isn't a spark, it's *chemoth*, heat that stays lit. You don't catch fire by standing near a match for one second. You catch it by standing near a furnace for months. That's what friendship with this kind of man actually is: slow exposure to a heat source you've stopped noticing is even on.

And that's exactly how *te'elaph* works — you *learn* his ways, which is a strange thing to say about anger, like it's a skill. But that's the honest word for it. Nobody studies to become short-tempered. It's absorbed, the same way kids pick up their parents' worst habits without ever being taught them on purpose. Spend enough car rides, enough lunch breaks, enough nights listening to him blow up at waiters or coworkers or his own family, and his reflexes slowly become your reflexes.

Which is why the proverb lands on *moqesh* — a snare, the exact device used to trap an animal that didn't see the danger coming. You don't walk into this friendship thinking "this will cost me my own temper." You walk in thinking you're just being a decent friend. The trap isn't obvious because it isn't baited with anything that looks dangerous. It's baited with company. That's the whole warning: watch who you let close enough to teach you something, because you're being taught whether you meant to sign up for the lesson or not.
